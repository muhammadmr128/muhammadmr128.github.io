Western Hillside Tailings Check

Four simple engineering checks on the numbers behind the Iron Ore Company of Canada's public plan to keep its tailings inside the lease line in Wabush Lake until 2038.

Independent practice exercise using public documents. Not an IOC or Rio Tinto design, and it uses no company data. Assumptions are clearly marked.

The problem

IOC deposits its tailings (the finely ground rock left after the iron is removed) in Wabush Lake, near Labrador City, and must keep them inside federal and provincial lease lines. Under the older 2006 plan, deposition would have crossed the federal lease line before the area was full.

The 2024 Western Hillside plan advances deposition west along the hillside, with five 20-inch coarse tailings pipelines and staged 5 m dyke raises, so the existing area can stay the only tailings facility until the end of 2038.

  • 34 Mt/yr design maximum tailings at 20 to 30% solids
  • 5 × 20-inch coarse tailings lines
  • 4,550 m of 5 m Central Control Dyke raises and extensions, 2025 to 2031

How tailings move

  1. MillOre is ground and the iron is separated; the rest is tailings.
  2. Slurry linesTailings are pumped as slurry, 20 to 30% solids by weight.
  3. Coarse beachSand settles near the outlet and is dozed into a beach.
  4. Control dykeCoarse tailings build the dyke that steers where fines go.
  5. WaterReleased water is clarified, then reclaimed or managed.

Four checks

Check 1

Storage

How much lake space does a year of tailings take?

volume = mass ÷ dry densityDry density: the mass of solids in each cubic metre once the tailings have settled.

  • 34,000,000 t ÷ 1.6 t/m³ = 21,250,000 m³ per year
  • Per day: 21,250,000 ÷ 365 ≈ 58,000 m³, about 23 Olympic pools a day (pool ≈ 2,500 m³)
  • At the actual 2025 production rate: 2025 concentrate was 16.9 Mt against a 23 Mt/yr design basis, so tailings ≈ 34 × 16.9 ÷ 23 ≈ 25 Mt, about 16 million m³/yr

Takeawayeven in the densest case it's about 18 million m³ a year, which is why the lease line drives the whole plan.

Check 2

Water

How much water arrives with the slurry?

  • At 25% solids, 100 t of slurry holds 25 t of solids and 75 t of water, so 75 ÷ 25 = 3 t of water per t of solids
  • 34 Mt × 3 = 102 Mt of water = 102 million m³ a year (1 t of water = 1 m³)
  • Water held in the pores: porosity (the share of settled volume that is empty space) n = 1 − 1.6 ÷ 3.0 = 0.47, so 21.25 × 0.47 ≈ 9.9 million m³
  • Released: 102 − 9.9 ≈ 92 million m³ a year (~90%), about 252,000 m³ a day
  • Range across the public 20 to 30% solids: 136 / 102 / 79 million m³ a year

Takeawayhandling the water is a bigger job than storing the solids.

Check 3

Dyke fill

How much material does one raise need?

From the registration: crest width 16.1 m, side slopes 2H:1V (2 m across for every 1 m up), each raise 5 m high.

  • Base width: 16.1 + 2 × (2 × 5) = 36.1 m
  • Cross-section area: (16.1 + 36.1) ÷ 2 × 5 = 130.5 m², so every metre of dyke needs 130.5 m³ of fill
  • 2025 raise of 800 m: 800 × 130.5 ≈ 104,400 m³, and × 1.6 t/m³ ≈ 167,000 t
  • Tailings made per day: 34,000,000 ÷ 365 ≈ 93,150 t, so the raise equals ≈ 1.8 days of production
  • All raises 2025 to 2031 (4,550 m) ≈ 594,000 m³ ≈ 10 days of production

Takeawaythe dyke is built from the waste itself, and there's always far more of it than needed: by-products as construction material.

Honest limit: a real raise sits partly on top of the old dyke, so this is an upper bound.

Check 4

Spring snowmelt

Does the freshet (the spring snowmelt flood) add a lot of water?

  • Snowfall 428 cm per year; assume 10 cm of snow ≈ 1 cm of water, so 428 ÷ 10 = 42.8 cm = 0.428 m of water
  • Exposed area 70.38 ha = 703,800 m²; runoff coefficient (the share of melt that runs off instead of soaking in) 0.8, since frozen ground soaks up little
  • 0.428 × 703,800 × 0.8 ≈ 241,000 m³ ≈ 0.24 million m³

Takeawaythe freshet adds little water; its real risk is erosion and sediment, which the registration names as the project's main environmental factor. About half of the year's 840 mm of precipitation falls as snow.

My question

Frozen fill

Coarse tailings placed near freezing can trap ice and settle on thaw. How is compaction checked in spring and fall?

My question

Freshet timing

Melt runs onto frozen ground over a few weeks. Are ditches and sumps ready before the thaw?

My question

Slurry lines in deep cold

Below zero from November to May. How are low points protected during a pump outage?

What I took away

  1. Storage is used at about 58,000 m³ a day, so lease-line planning drives the deposition plan.
  2. About 92 million m³ of slurry water is released each year and must be clarified and reclaimed or managed.
  3. Coarse tailings are the site's own building material; winter and the freshet decide when it can be placed.
